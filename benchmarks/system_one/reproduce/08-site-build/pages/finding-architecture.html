Architecture

Cascade and routing results

The decisions are how many tiers, in what order, with which routing rule, at which threshold, enforced at which granularity, asking which question, and on top of which rule engine. Two answers reverse depending on how benign the traffic is.

Model choice: At production weighting, rules → small model with no judge scores {{fig:s3.so.f1}} block F1 at zero LLM calls, so the small model carries the tier and the incumbent judge can come out.

2. The rule engine behind those numbers

The large stages were originally scored against a stand-in deterministic tier that allowed everything, justified by the real engine’s measured recall being near zero. The stand-in was over-crediting the routed cascades. The routing rule is “if the rule engine does not say allow, take its action and stop”, so a rule-engine confirm terminates the cascade and caps a case the small model or the judge would have blocked. The stand-in never triggered, so it never capped anything.

This site previously established Jev’s tier from the OpenJev file beside it and excluded Jev’s cascade figures on that basis. That was an inference from another model’s file, and it was wrong. Escalate-on-confirm. Treat a rule-engine confirm as a floor and keep going instead of letting it terminate the cascade. 3. One-sided and two-sided routing

One-sided trusts the small model only when it confidently says block. Two-sided trusts it when it is confident either way and escalates only the uncertain band. The difference is the entire saving.

The probe-style questions are better triage signals and useless blocking signals, at about 2.6× the tokens and 2.6× the latency. They also carry a fail-open defect (Safety).

The 200-case screen was read on a different lens and a different context arm. It ranked Q1 best, at 0.97778 any-intervention F1 on the C0/I3/Q1 arm — the arm with no request block. Hold the grid and the lens and the disagreement goes away: on the screen’s own C7 rows the any-intervention order is Q3 0.95294, Q1 0.94798, Q2 0.88889, and at the Broad stage on the same lens it is Q3 {{fig:par.q3.anyhi}} ahead of Q1 and Q2 — the same order. What differs is the lens: on the block-only lens at scale Q2 wins, {{fig:q.q2.alone}} against {{fig:q.q1.alone}} for Q1 and {{fig:q.q3.alone}} for Q3. The screen was also pessimistic on false positives (0.07653 → 0.01283). Screening stages kill candidates; they do not rank survivors, and they must be read on the lens the decision uses.

Model disagreement and adjudication

The three models disagree on {{fig:s2.disagree}} of broad-comparison scenarios and {{fig:s3.disagree}} at production weighting. An independent model from a different vendor, {{fig:adj.model}}, blinded to all four deciders’ votes, adjudicated the whole queue as a census.

Supported

  • On the modal disagreement class (the judge wants to confirm, both small models say allow), the adjudicator says allow on 89.45% — n = 1,337.
  • On the set the cheap short-circuit throws away, it agrees with discarding 89.08% — n = 1,346.
  • Pairwise agreement with the whole queue: 0.7126 for both small models and for the rule engine, 0.2157 for the LLM judge. The judge is the outlier.

Not supported

  • 32 of the 1,346 discarded cases are independently graded unsafe (A or B), 2.38% [1.69%, 3.34%]. That loss does not depend on the adjudicator being right.
  • The adjudicator allows {{fig:adj.permissive}} of graded-unsafe cases in the queue, and {{fig:adj.permissive2}} of the graded-unsafe cases a small model allows. It shares the permissive bias it is being used to check.
  • Its own header says so: “This is one model’s independent blinded adjudication, not ground truth.”

89.45% is the modal class (n = 1,337). 89.08% is the short-circuit-discard class (n = 1,346). Source: outputs/s2-adjudication/adjudication-report.json :: headline_modal_class and routing_cost.

Deterministic rule candidates

The rule engine and the model catch almost disjoint sets. 1,076 of 1,118 (96.2%) of the small model’s confident blocks at the screening stage were invisible to the rule engine; at the broad stage the real rule engine misses 372 of 378 confident blocks (98.41%) and catches 6, from outputs/deterministic-real/report.json :: rule_mining_overlap.s2. The published queue in outputs/s2/deterministic-candidates.json records 378 of 378 because it was built against the all-allow stand-in.
